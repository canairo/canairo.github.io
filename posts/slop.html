Introduction

An excerpt from an article by v12, wherein they describe their goals and motivations for their startup:

Our goal is to build a cyber nuke. That means building something that can find all of the bugs in a system. This is particularly interesting because such a capability would allow you to exploit software that’s previously considered secure. This hasn’t been possible until now, but frontier AI changes this.

v12's goal is to automate the process of vulnerability research from end to end. The product they wish to build is simple: it is a machine that can consume arbitrarily large programs and identify any exploits or bugs before attackers can, a giant can of pesticide they can spray at any repository so all the bugs -- regardless of complexity or obscurity -- die before the program ever becomes public. Simply, a giant fucking gun. A cyber nuke.

It is patently obvious that any company building a product at scale would find v12's "cyber nuke" to be a terribly desirable tool -- agents are now accessible to any person with a computer, malicious or not, and any company creating any sort of program would have to be keenly aware of how vulnerable they have now become. The grand democratization of vulnerability research now means that anybody could spend fifty dollars on GLM 5.3 tokens, point their own miniature "cyber nukes" at whatever they feel like, and compromise any program or server they wish. It is all terribly easy now.

Much hay has been made about these developments. I do not wish to add to that big pile of stuff. This is the new status quo, the cutting edge of vulnerability research as it stands, and countless thinkpieces, lamentations, paeans, celebratory LinkedIn posts, funereal dirges, blog posts, and assertations on valid use-cases have been written; this post is not that. Agentic AI is now frontier technology -- I am excited about it as anybody else is, and quietly mournful about (what seems to be) the obsolescence of vulnerability research as a craft. Posts about both of these things have all been written before.

My concern is something else. v12 has, in the past few months, published some of the results of their "cyber nuke". These are new kernel LPEs, RCEs in various database systems, a "guest-to-host" escape in Dolphin, so on and so forth; I can honestly say I have never seen the words "bidirectional" or "wormable" applied to exploits in the past. I think these are good pieces of work and I enjoy the further democratization of knowledge that they are providing here -- each proof-of-concept has been publicized, with an AI-generated writeup attached briefly going over the exploits.

As someone who does not have millions of dollars in venture capital money to spend on AI tokens, I appreciate that these proofs-of-concepts and writeups are public. I read through them whenever I can, and I earnestly try to learn something from each one I read. I wish to make myself clear: I am not some sort of Luddite or anti AI-warrior (although, I find myself far more sympathetic to the Luddites than I do the breathless AI-idolaters, they are usually more respectable and principled people). Agentic AI is capable of producing profound and wonderful work in vulnerability research; it is the only reason why v12's "cyber nuke" can exist in the first place.

The issue is this: if you want to build a cyber nuke, if you want to automate the entire process, there is no longer any need to make any of your work remotely comprehensible to humans. Writeups and blog posts, previously the lifeblood of cybersecurity and vulnerability research, are now completely unnecessary. These articles do not need to be made anymore. Vulnerability research, fundamentally, is results-oriented rather than knowledge-oriented: now that we have automated loops producing reliable results entirely without human intervention, no human troubleshooting or analysis is now required. The human is now entirely out of the loop. The results unilaterally speak for themselves, the calc.exe window is always going to appear regardless of whether or not someone understands why it appears.

If you are a company buying cybersecurity services, or if you are a company using agentic AI to provide cybersecurity services, this is patently a non-issue, because results are results. The big fucking gun now exists, you should use it because it would be deeply stupid not to. The main arguments against this are (as stated earlier) the obsolescence of the craft, and the death of the culture surrounding it. These, to speak mordantly, are entirely irrelevant if you want to make absurd amounts of money (I say this with entirely no judgment -- get that bag! Seriously!).

As it stands, humans are not wholly irrelevant, I doubt they fully will be. On some (increasingly thinner and thinner) level someone needs to understand how an exploit functions, whether it be the maintainer or the N-day developer who needs to iron out inconsistencies in whatever exploit Codex spits out. The gap of human expertise remains. People still need to get good at vulnerability research (even if what 'vulnerability research' is has drastically changed in the past year), and additionally, new people still need to get started.

Getting Started

It is -- as I am sure most who have tried know -- supremely difficult to get started in vulnerability research. Most learning binary exploitation or reverse engineering have to hone their craft through a thin network of publically available Internet resources: CTF writeups, blog posts and articles, GitHub repositories, the occasional doctoral thesis. Compare this with a prospective mathematics or computer science undergraduate: there are entire libraries you can consult, university programs you can enrol into, books entire centuries old.

The resources available are sparse. This is for a few reasons: the inherent difficulty of the field, the adjacency of the field to military applications, the origins of the field as an illegal practice, the nascency of the field as a whole. This is why when articles like Smashing the Stack for Fun and Profit dropped in 1996 on Phrack, or the Malloc Maleficarum in 2005, they were passed around on old BBS forums like priceless samizdat. These papers, little kernels of knowledge passed through the information superhighway (back when they were still calling it the information superhighway), necessarily had to remain under the radar.

Resources are more available now: pwn.college and Dreamhack exist, Google Project Zero make their work public, and you can just sit down and watch DEFCON talks on YouTube. Still, actually trying to get your start in vulnerability research remains difficult: the sort of thing only a dedicated, lonely teenager with too much time to spare gets into. Part of this is because the skill itself is difficult to learn, most of it is because no one is teaching you how to. Vulnerability research, in the past, was a seldom-trodden path you get inducted into like a domestic terrorist cell or a cult.

The existence of the AI-generated writeup now stands at odds with this. Here we have this great big machine, speaking the language of angels and operating at speeds thought unthinkable only a year beforehand. The grand robot. Everyone can now use this big fucking gun, and the big fucking gun can now automatically explain itself -- here is the weak spot in the program's armor, here is my .44 caliber love letter straight into its heart, here is the calc.exe or the reverse shell or the touch /tmp/pwned, what have you. Here is all human information about vulnerability research, collated from sparse, isolated tendrils of the Internet all into one coagulated, pulsating mass, and now we can produce countless technical articles, research papers, blog posts, CTF writeups, learning materials about it all. This is what v12 does when it produces a WRITEUP.md for its wormable MariaDB exploit, this is what Christopher Domas' agent did when it wrote about skitter-creek-bath-salts. This is what every cybersecurity lab does when it publishes some new RCE in some new program on their blog. An utter wellspring of information-coated black goo, put your hands in and drink from it.

Ostensibly, this should usher in an exciting new age of vulnerability research education. I can refresh my Twitter page and get a new RCE writeup like they're TikToks about the TV show Suits. There are more writeups than I could ever possibly read, a hummingbird could scarcely keep up. This feels to me how academics, researchers, and even the common layperson must have felt when the Internet was coming to fruition -- libraries worth of information at your fingertips! Everyone will become polymaths! A textbook or a movie or an academic paper with the click of a button!

The task of training new vulnerability researchers is now solved, right? No longer does cybersecurity information need to be passed around and communicated through an abstruse system of telephone, anyone who wants to learn about iOS kernel exploitation or absurd ROP techniques can just consult, like, Grokipedia, or whatever. This is the end stage of the grand machine's universal democratization of vulnerability research: anyone can now learn these things, and it is no longer the domain of lonely teenagers trying to hack GTA: San Andreas, learning about WinDBG in the process. Great, right?

The problem is that it all sucks.

It All Sucks

Now we get to my real problem -- the countless unbearable issues about AI writeups, the overall inefficacy of AI as a mentor, and the second-order effects of the exclusion of humans from the field as a whole. Many of these will be annoying and nitpicking gripes about style and language, some of these will be more poignant and worthwhile experiences stemming from my experience as a cybersecurity educator, and a lot of this will be pointless complaining.

I so often wonder -- does anyone actually read this shit? Am I going crazy? There are so many writeups being produced and so many of them are immensely technically valuable but so fucking annoying. Clearly, the underlying concepts and findings are sound, but the manner in which they are delivered make it utterly fucking impossible to comprehend, understand, or even tolerate. It simply escapes me how anyone can bear to read this stuff. Is any human actually gaining anything from this?

I present some choice excerpts:

The trick: be fast, and don't touch DRAM. Disable the APs, prime the TLBs, warm the cache, disable interrupts, flush the target, serialize memory accesses, and hope the CPU prefetched the upcoming instructions. Then rewire the MCT/DCT to spaghettify DRAM, grab some data from the protected region, revert the mappings, serialize again, enable interrupts, resume the APs, and the platform is back to normal.

The above is from Christopher Domas' skitter-creek-bath-salts. The exploit flies above my head entirely. It is something about modifying DRAM. I submit: half of this is because I do not understand CPUs at a low enough level, and half of it is because this is written like nothing else on Earth. It is part breathless advertising copy, part condescending Redditor-speak, part unexplained technical jargon and nomenclature, and all obnoxious. This DRAM wizardry is probably the coolest thing in the world -- my friends all seem to think so -- and yet as a writeup it is stylistically nails-on-a-chalkboard. It feels like the agent is trying to sell me a car. It feels like Primer dialogue. It feels fucking annoying.

Christopher Domas is an excellent science communicator and a profound inspiration for most in the field. His work on the sand-sifter and movfuscator are groundbreaking pieces of work, and his presentations on them are fantastic, compelling, and easy to understand. I have so much respect for Christopher Domas, and his work is consistently innovative in a field where it is already so difficult to push the envelope. I wish he would write his work himself because he is good at writing and I can understand him when he does. I cannot understand his agent at all. His agent, an indispensable assistant to his already valuable skillset, should not be talking for him because his agent fucking sucks at talking.

This is not a new observation. The latest enemy of the vibecoding software engineer is Claudespeak, this bizarre, winding acrolect or glossolalia like a Sphinx telling you its riddles three. Let's smoke-test the load-bearing flagging-but-not-verifying null-check. Utterly fucking unreadable! I like to think I am literate and good at understanding things, sometimes Claudespeak makes me doubt this.

Exactly—the current implementation is heroically imperative. The historically-flaky config surface is inferred—the snapshots (flagged, not fixed) are a place where I'd accept a different answer.

I am not alone in finding Claude-alect unintelligible:

Something's off with Opus 5 - it tends to give me overly long replies, use Claude-speak way too much (e.g., "here's the honest truth"), and is too judgemental. Opus used to be a joy to talk to like a trusted friend. Not so much anymore.

Have you noticed that Claude no longer speaks English? It speaks a jargon-filled AI dialect where every sentence sounds like a zen riddle.

opus 4.6 is the last anthropic model that i had no problem reading the writing of quickly. all of the models since, incl fable, are a lot less legible. for both coding and convo. is it an RL artifact? what's causing it? i've seen this sentiment shared by friends

Most of these Tweets are, however, from the most illiterate class of people ever (Y-Combinator startup hopefuls) so I would take this with a grain of salt, but I think the issue persists.

The problem is not merely stylistic; if Claudem Ipsum's only problem was insufferableness, I would suck it up and deal with it. The problem is that it is terrible as a science communicator, and lacks all the characteristics you would want from a technical writer. Technical writers are succinct, emotionless, clear, and maximize information throughput above all else. They are dry, but pleasant to read, and I am actually able to learn things from a good (or even mediocre) technical writer. LLMs do not do this. LLMs seem to actively obfuscate information now. I feel like I have to cut into the technical writeups of an LLM and spread apart its organs on a wooden table like a haruspex to gain anything at all.

Speaking of organs:

Anatomy

There is a peculiar anatomy to how AI writeups about exploits are delivered.

  1. There is an unevenness to their explanations -- they fixate on trivialities that any competent reader would already understand, and leave critical and interesting points unexplained.
  2. They are constantly trying to sell you on the exploit, as if the exploit does not already sell itself. This is done through reiterations of the specific mitigations avoided, for instance.
  3. Their approach is entirely different from a human's, so information that is actually useful to a human (debugging setup, path of discovery, setup) is omitted.
  4. They often invent new, non-standard technical terminology, treating these words as if they are common and do not need explaining.
  5. They are smarmy and condescending.

Here are some examples. The first is from Synthreats' AI-generated writeup on an RCE in MariaDB.

Command execution from an account with no privileges. The entry account holds only GRANT USAGE ON *.*, the MariaDB equivalent of connect-only access. From that account and a TCP session to port 3306, the chain reaches system() running as uid=999(mysql), with no shell access, no host tooling, and no prior knowledge of any memory address.

You're telling me you can achieve RCE with 'no shell access, no host tooling, and no prior knowledge of any memory address'? No shit buddy, that's what an RCE is! There is no point explaining that this RCE requires 'no shell access', and additionally, the understanding that the account has no privileges is entirely implicit.

The exploitation chain is placed in memory using SQL string functions. A 128 MiB user variable, REPEAT(CHAR(0xDE), 134217728), forces glibc to return a dedicated mmap region. The fake virtual-function table and the two gadgets are embedded into that buffer at allocation time using CONCAT and UNHEX, and the buffer's address is located by differencing two /proc/self/maps snapshots from within the database.

Important information here is mixed with entirely irrelevant detail. The mmapped chunk is an important exploit detail -- yes, intentionally allocating a large chunk so that it is mmapped is a known exploitation technique, but the fact that it relies on "CONCAT" and "UNHEX"? Who cares, dude?

This analysis reproduced the chain end to end on an isolated lab host against the stock, unmodified mariadb@sha256:ef34af04...fa7b3e9 Docker image (13.0.1-MariaDB-ubu2604). Both attempts succeeded, each against a freshly randomised address-space layout.

A 'freshly randomised address-space layout'? It's PIE. You are defeating PIE. Defeating PIE is something that most exploits need already. No human would ever talk about PIE in this way. Everybody would just say something along the lines of "the exploit uses an OOB read to get a PIE leak", and anyone trying to understand the workings of the bug would immediately understand it. This, to me, is specifically stymieing: this explanation and rephrasal of PIE is entirely meant for nobody. A person without relevant technical background does not even know that ASLR exists, so it is not worth trying to explain this detail to them, and a person who wants to understand the exploit does not need the concept of PIE explained to them in this manner.

It is difficult to specifically critique AI writeups without sounding like a curmudgeon but I hope to make it clear that when every single sentence of every single writeup is written like this, with irrelevant detail, unusual terminology, and bizarre and redundant verbiage, it becomes so difficult to actually gain anything worthwhile. I am so outspoken and annoying about this because it makes my job so much harder. My attempt at explaining this bug goes something like this: "A realloc() causes a use-after-free, which you can use to overwrite a vtable for RIP hijacking". The concept of vtable overriding, a use-after-free, the realloc() function, and RIP hijacking are all well understood, and if I discussed this with one of my friends, they would immediately understand the bug.

They would further understand me when I say something like, "you need a lot of heap feng shui to make sure the chunks for leaks are set-up correctly", because I am a human and I understand the average sticking points for human-led binary exploitation: heap layouts, offset configuration, actually navigating the state of the chunks and bins in a debugger. The LLM's main sticking points are instead things like PIE, for whatever arcane reason, so it devotes a thousand different lines to explaining how it 'defeats PIE'.

Theory of Mind

The primary reason for this unintelligibility is that agentic AI fundamentally approaches vulnerability research differently from how a human does, and it is therefore unable to effectively explain its findings to a human because of this difference in methodology. It does not know what is worth focusing on and what is uninteresting, it does not understand how humans operate or learn, and it cannot put itself in a human's shoes. It will never need the bata24 fork of gef because it can just read /proc/self/mem.

The result of all of this nonsense is the co-opting of the medium of the 'writeup' as something entirely anti-human, text with literally zero audience. Art for no one. This difference is not something you can prompt your way out of, it is a feature of this big machine you cannot cut out, at least not right now. There is little incentive to make these writeups any more intelligible in the first place -- for the most part, the cyber nuke does not need human operators.

A brief aside to illustrate my point. Here is a post about AI in mathematics. Mathematics, another field where breathless AI-ideologues and startup-founders manqué gleefully pass around writeups and AI-generated explanations they cannot read, is simultaneously similar to and entirely different from cybersecurity in many ways vis-à-vis AI influence. The central argument of this (very well-written) article is that proving the nth Erdős problem is useless because mathematics is not about proving theorems, it is about contributing to the font of mathematical human knowledge. Theorems and proofs are a byproduct of this ever-growing system of ideas, which forms the actual beating heart of mathematics. AI's capacity to prove unsolved theorems is particularly politically useful for AI ideologues because it, at a glance, is unmistakable evidence of AI's ability to replace humans in even the most intellectually challenging fields, but it is entirely useless to actual mathematicians because the proofs are often useless: they do not add anything to the widespread human understanding of mathematics as it exists.

Perhaps you see the problem here.
